# Professional identity exposure

Professional visibility is useful.

It helps people find you, understand what you do and build trust.

It can also help an attacker understand who you are, who you know, what you can access and how to approach you.

The objective is not to disappear from the Internet.

It is to control what is visible, to whom, and for how long.

## 1. Think in attack paths

Do not review a professional profile as a collection of isolated fields.

Ask what someone could understand by combining them, such as collecting enouth data to impersonate your identity and to betray someone's trust

A profile, post or public document may reveal:

- your role and level of authority;
- privileged or sensitive responsibilities;
- reporting lines and trusted relationships;
- customers, suppliers and partners;
- technologies and security products;
- office locations;
- events and travel;
- professional email patterns;
- phone numbers;
- links to personal accounts;
- information about colleagues or family.

One detail may be harmless.

Several details combined may create an attack path.

> Information does not need to be secret to become sensitive when it is aggregated.

## 2. Understand relationship levels

Professional networks are not simple public or private spaces.

On LinkedIn, for example, information may be exposed differently to:

- 1st-degree connections;
- 2nd-degree connections;
- the wider LinkedIn network;
- people who are not signed in;
- search engines;
- permitted third-party services.

A connection therefore represents more than a contact.

It may change what that person can see about you and your network.

Review connection requests accordingly.

Before accepting someone, ask:

- Do I know this person?
- Is there a clear professional reason to connect?
- Is the profile consistent with the claimed identity?
- Does the account have a credible history and network?
- What additional information will this connection gain access to?

A large network can be professionally useful.

It can also expose more of the trust relationships around you.

## 3. Review privacy settings

Do not rely on default settings.

Review them periodically because platforms and your own needs change.

On LinkedIn, pay particular attention to:

- public profile visibility;
- which profile sections are visible publicly;
- off-LinkedIn visibility;
- discoverability by email address;
- discoverability by phone number;
- email-address visibility;
- whether connections can download your email address;
- visibility of your connections;
- profile-viewing mode;
- activity status;
- profile updates and notifications to your network.

The right setting depends on why you use the platform.

A recruiter, salesperson, security engineer and executive may need different levels of visibility.

Use the minimum exposure that still provides the professional value you need.

## 4. Your network is information

Connections can reveal organizational structure.

They may help identify:

- executives;
- assistants;
- administrators;
- security teams;
- IT staff;
- suppliers;
- consultants;
- recruiters;
- former employees;
- customers;
- people with access to sensitive environments.

An attacker may not approach the most senior or most protected person directly.

Someone around them may provide an easier route.

Comments, recommendations, mentions and tagged photographs can reveal relationships even when the connection list itself is hidden.

Think about the graph, not only the profile.

## 5. Separate professional and private exposure

Professional visibility should not automatically expose private life.

Where practical:

- use separate professional and personal email addresses;
- avoid publishing a private phone number;
- review whether the same username is reused across unrelated platforms;
- avoid linking professional profiles unnecessarily to personal accounts;
- limit public information about family members;
- avoid publishing a precise home location;
- review photographs for addresses, badges, screens, documents or identifiable locations.

Complete separation is not always possible.

The goal is to reduce easy correlation.

## 6. Do not assume hidden contact details are undiscoverable

Removing an email address or telephone number from a social profile does not guarantee that it cannot be found elsewhere.

Contact-data and sales-intelligence services aggregate professional information from multiple sources.

Linkedin Third party services may associate professional profiles with email addresses, telephone numbers and other contact information.

Other services, can find professional email addresses from public sources or infer likely addresses from known email patterns used by an organization and then attempt to verify them.

Information may also remain available through:

- company websites;
- press releases;
- conference pages;
- public documents;
- professional directories;
- historical pages;
- search-engine indexes;
- commercial datasets.

This changes the defensive assumption.

> Your contact details do not need to be explicitly published to become discoverable.

Privacy settings reduce exposure.

They do not guarantee invisibility.

## 7. Think before publishing technical details

Professional credibility often involves talking about technology.

The problem is not mentioning technology.

The problem is unintentionally describing the target environment.

Consider the value of publishing:

- security products in use;
- firewall or EDR vendors;
- internal architecture;
- network segmentation;
- cloud providers;
- identity platforms;
- remote-access technologies;
- migration projects;
- vulnerabilities currently being addressed;
- photographs of technical environments.

A technology name alone may mean little.

Technology + organization + role + project timing can mean much more.

Share enough to demonstrate expertise without unnecessarily documenting the environment for someone else.

## 8. Avoid real-time operational disclosure

Timing can transform ordinary information into useful intelligence.

Examples include:

- announcing that you are currently travelling;
- identifying a customer site while you are there;
- publishing the location of a confidential meeting;
- showing that an executive team is away;
- posting photographs of access badges;
- showing meeting-room names or whiteboards;
- announcing security work while it is in progress.

When there is no operational reason to publish immediately, consider publishing afterwards.

The professional value is often the same.

The exposure is not.

## 9. Search for yourself

Periodically look at yourself from the outside.

Search using combinations such as:

- name;
- name + employer;
- name + role;
- name + city;
- professional email address;
- telephone number;
- usernames;
- previous employers.

Do not stop at the first search result.

Look for relationships between results.

Ask:

- What can someone learn about my authority?
- What can they infer about my access?
- Who appears to trust me?
- Which technologies are associated with me?
- Can my professional identity be linked to my private life?
- Can someone determine where I live or where I will be?
- Can old information still be found?
- What would make an impersonation attempt more convincing?

The useful output is not a list of search results.

It is a list of unnecessary exposures that can be reduced.

## 10. Search engines remember differently

Changing or deleting information on a website does not necessarily remove it immediately from search results.

Search engines may continue to display previously indexed information until they crawl the source again.

When possible:

1. remove or correct the information at the original source;
2. wait for or request an index refresh where appropriate;
3. check whether the result is still associated with your name;
4. use available removal or de-referencing procedures where justified.

In the European Union, the right to de-referencing can allow a person, under certain conditions, to ask a search engine to stop associating specific results with searches for their name.

De-referencing is not deletion.

The original information may remain available on the source website.

Reducing exposure therefore requires looking at both the source and the search engine.

## 11. AI changes the scale of correlation

The risk is no longer limited to what a person can manually find on one website.

Public AI services can synthesize and correlate information from many sources.

MITRE ATT&CK describes this behavior as **T1682 — Query Public AI Services**.

Information that appears independently across professional profiles, websites, documents, news, code repositories and public databases can be combined into a more complete picture of a person or organization.

This may reveal:

- organizational structures;
- key personnel;
- technologies;
- business relationships;
- likely contact information;
- professional interests;
- writing patterns;
- plausible reasons for contacting someone.

AI can also reduce the effort required to turn that information into tailored content.

MITRE ATT&CK **T1683 — Generate Content** covers content created to support activities such as impersonation, social engineering and fraud, including AI-assisted written and audio-visual content.

This can make approaches more credible:

- a message written in the vocabulary of the target's industry;
- a fake recruiter who understands the person's career;
- a supplier pretext using real project information;
- an executive impersonation using known relationships;
- a message imitating expected professional context;
- synthetic audio, images or video supporting an impersonation attempt.

The defensive question is therefore no longer only:

> What have I published?

It is also:

> What can be inferred when everything public about me is combined?

## 12. Do not trust familiarity alone

A convincing message is not proof of identity.

Knowing your employer, manager, project, customers, colleagues or recent activity may simply demonstrate good reconnaissance.

For unusual or sensitive requests:

- verify through another trusted channel;
- do not rely only on information contained in the message;
- be particularly careful with payment, credential, access and reset requests;
- treat urgency and secrecy as reasons to verify, not reasons to skip verification.

The more public information exists, the easier it becomes to manufacture familiarity.

## 13. Review periodically

Professional exposure changes over time.

A reasonable review should include:

- profile visibility;
- privacy settings;
- connection visibility;
- contact information;
- search-engine results;
- old biographies;
- company pages;
- conference appearances;
- public documents;
- code repositories;
- third-party contact-data services;
- links between professional and private identities.

Do not try to remove everything.

Prioritize information that helps create a credible attack path.

Keep what provides professional value.

Reduce, restrict or remove what only increases exposure.
