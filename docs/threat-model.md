# Threat model

Imagine that someone is building a short target brief before making contact or attempting access.

The review asks one question:

> Which public facts make a harmful action easier, safer or more credible?

## Five information families

| Information | What it can reveal | Example consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Identity | names, accounts, email patterns | account discovery or impersonation |
| Authority | decisions, approvals, privileged roles | fraud or pressure on colleagues |
| Relationships | assistants, suppliers, relatives, reporting lines | trusted pretexts or indirect targeting |
| Places | office, home area, venue, transport | unwanted contact or predictable transitions |
| Timing | travel, leave, events, routines | a better moment for an approach |

## Priority people

| Person | Why exposure matters |
| --- | --- |
| Executives | authority, public visibility and financial decisions |
| Administrators | direct access to critical systems and recovery paths |
| Privileged employees | access to sensitive data, processes or facilities |
| Assistants and close collaborators | trusted relationships, schedules and escalation paths |

Priority rises when three elements meet:

1. the person can influence a valuable resource;
2. enough public information exists to build a credible approach;
3. the likely consequence is serious.

## Boundaries

Use only public or authorized sources. Do not test a person, follow them, contact relatives, access private accounts or purchase illicit data.

The output is not a personality profile. It is a short list of exposed facts, plausible consequences and practical changes.
